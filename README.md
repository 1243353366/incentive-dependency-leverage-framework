# The Incentive–Dependency–Leverage Strategic Analysis Framework

**Integrated strategic edition · Layers 0–23 · Three global trump cards · DARVO and elicitation protocols · Strategic intervention sequence · Final decision gate**

## Purpose

A structured framework for analyzing human behavior, conflicts, negotiations, institutions, interpersonal dynamics, and strategic decisions through incentives, dependencies, leverage, information, relational drivers, evidence, and consequences.

The objective is not to prove that someone is manipulative, predict every action, or win every interaction. It is to understand what the evidence supports, identify realistic options, protect legitimate interests, and choose the best action at an acceptable cost.

Strategic intent is a hypothesis, not a default explanation. Every intervention must remain defensible if the preferred interpretation is wrong.

# Foundational model

Four interacting variables form the core of the framework:

- **Incentives:** What outcomes reward or punish each party?
- **Dependencies:** What resources, relationships, or conditions does each party rely on?
- **Leverage:** What can each party realistically influence, provide, withhold, change, document, or refuse—and at what cost?
- **Behavior:** What do people actually do within those conditions, and what evidence supports an explanation?

| Concept | Definition | Critical distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Incentive | A consequence that makes an action more or less attractive | Benefit does not prove motive |
| Dependency | Reliance on a resource, relationship, person, or system | Dependence does not prove exploitation |
| Leverage | Capacity to influence an outcome through available resources or options | Potential influence does not guarantee control |
| Power | Capacity to shape decisions, access, behavior, or outcomes | Power is neutral; its use has consequences |
| Constraint | A condition limiting available actions | A nominal choice may carry substantial costs |
| Behavioral driver | A factor that may help explain behavior, such as habit, affection, fear, or identity | A plausible explanation is not an established cause |

These variables interact, but none independently establishes intent or guarantees an outcome.

# Part I — Map the system

## Layer 0 — Reality, Evidence, and Uncertainty

**Core question: What do I actually know?**

Classify information as:

- **Observed:** Directly witnessed or recorded.
- **Corroborated:** Supported by independent evidence or sources.
- **Inferred:** Reasonably derived from established facts.
- **Hypothesized:** Plausible but unverified.
- **Unknown:** Missing, ambiguous, or unresolved.

Examine source reliability, chronology, missing context, source independence, and evidence that could contradict the current interpretation.

For interpersonal conflict, distinguish the original event from the subsequent argument about that event. Record exact wording where possible, but preserve enough surrounding context to avoid misleading excerpts.

**Output:** Fact base, confidence assessment, uncertainty list, and verification plan.

## Layer 1 — Objective and Incentive Mapping

**Core question: What does everyone want?**

Assess stated objectives, observable priorities, rewards, penalties, competing interests, short- and long-term incentives, and changes across audiences or circumstances.

Ask what would happen if the incentives were reversed. Consider whether the same behavior fits several possible motives.

Do not assume that a person who benefits from an outcome deliberately engineered it.

**Output:** Provisional incentive map with competing explanations.

## Layer 2 — Dependency Mapping

**Core question: What does everyone depend on?**

Identify material, relational, informational, institutional, and operational dependencies.

For each dependency, determine who supplies it, whether alternatives exist, the cost of switching, the consequences of losing access, and whether reliance is voluntary or difficult to escape.

In interpersonal disputes, a need for acknowledgment or closure can become a dependency if progress is made contingent on the other person admitting fault. However, legitimate needs for safety, accountability, restitution, or procedural fairness should not be dismissed as mere emotional dependence.

**Output:** Dependency map showing bottlenecks, alternatives, and vulnerabilities.

## Layer 3 — Leverage and Power Mapping

**Core question: What can everyone realistically influence?**

Assess control over resources, information, cooperation, credibility, institutional processes, time, and exit options.

For every potential source of leverage, assess:

- Availability.
- Credibility.
- Practical and lawful usability.
- Whether it can be bypassed.
- Cost to both parties.
- Relevance to the desired outcome.
- Proportionality and unintended consequences.

Distinguish the ability to influence an outcome from the ability merely to cause harm.

**Output:** Realistic capability map, limitations, costs, and potential consequences.

## Layer 4 — Options, Alternatives, and Exit Costs

**Core question: What can each party do without the other's cooperation?**

Assess viable alternatives, outside options, switching costs, reversibility, and the consequences of leaving, refusing, delaying, negotiating, or continuing.

Ask whether reducing a dependency is more effective than increasing leverage.

A person's ability to leave may improve their bargaining position, but exit is not automatically cheap, safe, or desirable.

**Output:** Options map identifying the least costly viable alternatives.

## Layer 5 — Bargaining, Reciprocity, and Commitments

**Core question: What is being exchanged, promised, required, or withheld?**

Map explicit and implicit obligations, offers, concessions, deadlines, acceptable outcomes, walk-away conditions, and who bears the risk of failure.

Define:

1. The requested outcome.
2. The relevant terms and responsibilities.
3. How completion will be verified.
4. What happens if the agreement is not met.
5. What conditions justify continuing, renegotiating, or exiting.

Do not confuse an agreement to stop arguing with an agreement to resolve the underlying issue.

**Output:** Clear terms, verification criteria, boundaries, and continuation or exit conditions.

# Part II — Information, influence, and adversarial behavior

## Layer 6 — Information Asymmetry and Control

**Core question: Who knows what, who can verify it, and who controls access?**

Examine information advantages, source quality, access to original records, disclosure incentives, communication channels, timing, and independent verification.

Missing information may result from ignorance, limited access, privacy, memory, poor communication, or deliberate withholding.

Information control is not automatically manipulation. People can legitimately retain private information or decline to answer questions they are not obligated to answer.

**Output:** Information map and verification plan.

## Layer 7 — Framing, Narrative, and Reputation

**Core question: Who is defining the problem, and what does that framing make easier or harder?**

Compare how parties describe the situation, which facts they emphasize or omit, how responsibility is assigned, and who benefits from a particular narrative.

Construct a neutral statement of the problem and identify claims that can be tested.

A useful neutral format is:

- What happened?
- What evidence supports that account?
- What remains disputed?
- What resolution is being requested?

**Output:** Competing narratives, testable claims, and a neutral framing.

## Layer 8 — Manipulation, Provocation, and Counter-Manipulation

**Core question: Is anyone attempting to influence behavior through pressure, distortion, or strategically structured interaction?**

Examine observable patterns such as moving goalposts, misleading framing, repeated contradictions, coercive urgency, selective disclosure, blame-shifting, and attempts to make discussion of the complainant's conduct replace discussion of the original complaint.

For each suspected tactic, identify the behavior, possible incentive, relevant dependency, potential leverage, alternative explanations, and supporting evidence.

Defensive responses include slowing decisions, documenting facts, verifying claims, setting boundaries, clarifying terms, and using legitimate processes.

### DARVO as a specific pattern

DARVO stands for **Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender**. It describes a pattern in which a person accused of harmful conduct denies or minimizes the allegation, attacks the person raising it, and recasts themselves as the injured party.

The analytical question is not whether someone denied an allegation or complained about the accuser. Either can be legitimate. The question is whether the sequence repeatedly displaces examination of the original allegation and prevents meaningful resolution.

Assess:

- What was the original allegation?
- What response was given to the actual allegation?
- Did the discussion shift toward the complainant's tone, motives, or character?
- Is the counter-complaint independently relevant and supported?
- Does the original issue remain unresolved after the shift?
- Does the pattern recur in comparable situations?

### Defensive protocol: preserve the original issue

**1. State the original question in one neutral sentence.**

Example: "Did we agree that X would happen, and was X completed?"

**2. Separate the two issues.**

Example: "We can discuss my tone separately. I still need an answer about whether X happened."

This does not mean ignoring legitimate complaints about your conduct. It means neither issue automatically erases the other.

**3. Name the observable shift, if useful, without turning the label into the argument.**

Example: "We're now discussing how I raised the issue rather than whether it happened. Can we address both separately?"

Naming DARVO itself is optional. A procedural description of the behavior may be more effective and less inflammatory.

**4. Return to the evidence.**

Use records, dates, specific commitments, and clearly defined terms. Do not demand agreement with your interpretation as a substitute for examining evidence.

**5. Decide what happens if the issue remains unresolved.**

Use proportionate boundaries, independent review, legitimate formal processes, or disengagement where appropriate.

The aim is to keep the original issue answerable—not to force an admission or prove that the other person is malicious.

**Output:** Behavior-based assessment and proportionate response.

## Layer 9 — Elicitation and Information-Gathering

**Core question: How can I clarify a hypothesis without contaminating the evidence?**

Elicitation is the process of obtaining information through questions, conversation, prompts, or interaction. It can be benign, professional, strategic, manipulative, or some combination depending on context and conduct.

An information asymmetry exists when one party knows something the other does not. That fact alone does not prove exploitation.

### Four useful clarification methods

1. **Correction trigger:** Offer a tentative, neutral summary and invite correction.
2. **Discrepancy clarification:** Ask how a claim fits with conflicting evidence.
3. **Bracketing:** Clarify approximate timing, quantities, or conditions.
4. **Recap testing:** Summarize an account and ask whether it is accurate.

Avoid deliberately misrepresenting another person's position just to see what they correct. A false summary can introduce confusion, provoke defensiveness, and contaminate the evidence.

A correction establishes that a correction was made. It does not independently establish the full account, the truth of every detail, or the person's motive.

### Defensive protocol: manage your own information disclosure

When a conversation feels probing or high-stakes:

- Pause before answering.
- Answer the question actually asked, rather than volunteering an extended narrative.
- Ask why information is needed when the purpose is unclear.
- Distinguish what you know from what you suspect.
- Avoid guessing to fill silence.
- Keep appropriate personal boundaries.
- Verify the other person's account rather than treating their confidence as proof.
- Preserve a reliable timeline for your own use.

**Short answers are not a universal rule.** A detailed answer may be necessary for a fair investigation, a professional responsibility, or a legitimate attempt to resolve a dispute. The goal is deliberate, accurate disclosure—not reflexive secrecy.

Likewise, do not conceal information you are legally or contractually required to provide, mislead an investigator, or obstruct a legitimate process.

**Output:** Statements, corrections, unresolved discrepancies, disclosure decisions, and verification needs.

## Layer 10 — Interaction Effects and Evidence Contamination

**Core question: How might the interaction itself have shaped the behavior or information I am interpreting?**

Account for wording, pressure, audience, timing, assumptions embedded in questions, repeated questioning, and your own contribution.

Separate what happened before, during, and after the interaction.

A reaction such as anger, silence, nervousness, withdrawal, or defensiveness may have multiple explanations. Your own calmness, insistence, withdrawal, or questioning can also influence the response.

Do not interpret a reaction you helped produce as independent proof of the theory that motivated your intervention.

**Output:** Interaction-adjusted assessment with explicit uncertainty.

## Layer 11 — Cognitive Dissonance and Behavioral Inconsistency

**Core question: Where do statements, actions, incentives, and constraints fail to align?**

Compare stated values with repeated conduct, promises with follow-through, explanations with records, and claimed constraints with demonstrated alternatives.

Consider deception, changed circumstances, competing priorities, memory errors, ambivalence, and poor execution as competing explanations.

A contradiction may prompt reconsideration, but it does not guarantee behavioral change. A person may revise beliefs, reinterpret evidence, accept the inconsistency, avoid the issue, or change their actions.

**Output:** Concrete discrepancies, possible explanations, and evidence needed to distinguish them.

## Layer 12 — Counter-Elicitation and Attention Control

**Core question: Is someone shaping what I notice, disclose, assume, or investigate—and could I be doing the same?**

Examine leading questions, selective attention, redirection, premature disclosure requests, and hypotheses repeated until they feel established.

Ask:

- Who introduced the hypothesis?
- What evidence existed before the hypothesis was introduced?
- What information is the other person actually requesting?
- Is the question relevant to resolving the issue?
- Am I answering beyond what is necessary?
- Am I steering the other person toward a desired admission?
- Would an ordinary explanation fit the interaction?

When appropriate, narrow the discussion to one concrete, answerable issue rather than trying to resolve every accusation simultaneously.

**Output:** Assessment of how attention, assumptions, and disclosure may have been shaped.

## Layer 13 — Mirror Test and Self-Interest Audit

**Core question: What am I contributing to this situation, and what incentives distort my own judgment?**

Audit your objectives, dependencies, leverage, emotional stakes, information gaps, reputational concerns, and desire to be right.

Ask:

- Am I pursuing resolution or retaliation?
- Am I applying the same standards to myself and others?
- Did my conduct escalate the interaction?
- Am I denying a legitimate point while attacking how it was raised?
- Am I eliciting information to understand the issue or to win?
- Am I collecting evidence fairly, including material that weakens my own position?
- Would a less costly response protect my interests?
- Would I choose the same action if nobody else knew about it?

Two people can engage in defensive blame-shifting at the same time. Identifying one person's conduct does not excuse the other's.

**Output:** Self-audit distinguishing legitimate objectives from ego, retaliation, fear, or the desire to win.

## Layer 14 — Adversarial Theory Testing

**Core question: What if my interpretation is wrong, or a different explanation fits better?**

Test at least three explanations where appropriate:

1. **Strategic:** The person deliberately uses a behavior to influence the outcome.
2. **Ordinary:** The behavior reflects misunderstanding, habit, defensiveness, poor communication, or emotional reactivity.
3. **Mixed:** The person has a legitimate grievance while also behaving defensively or unfairly.

Evaluate each against chronology, independent evidence, incentives, dependencies, leverage, and contradictory evidence.

Ask what evidence would distinguish the explanations. Do not infer deliberate strategy solely because a behavior benefits the person displaying it.

A defensive response that works across multiple explanations is preferable to one that succeeds only if the most hostile interpretation is correct.

**Output:** Ranked hypotheses, confidence levels, and revision conditions.

## Layer 15 — Time, Sequence, and Changing Incentives

**Core question: What changed, when did it change, and what does the timing establish?**

Build a chronology of events, communications, knowledge, changing incentives, dependencies, options, and behavior.

Check whether a party knew enough at the time to act as hypothesized. Distinguish chronological sequence from causation.

Preserve relevant messages and records in context. A timeline can clarify contradictions, but chronology alone cannot establish intent.

**Output:** Evidence-linked timeline with causal claims separated from facts.

## Layer 16 — Strategic Outcome and Risk Analysis

**Core question: Which action best serves the legitimate objective at an acceptable cost?**

Compare possible actions by expected effectiveness, financial and relational costs, legal and reputational risks, reversibility, effects on dependencies, and consequences for uninvolved people.

Possible actions include:

- Do nothing.
- Ask for clarification.
- Preserve the original question.
- Separate competing complaints.
- Document and verify.
- Establish clear terms.
- Reduce dependency.
- Negotiate.
- Seek independent advice or review.
- Use a legitimate formal process.
- Disengage temporarily or permanently.

Prefer the smallest proportionate action that preserves flexibility and remains reasonable if your interpretation is wrong.

**Output:** Preferred action, alternatives, risk mitigation, and stopping conditions.

# Part III — Strategic intervention

This sequence translates the analytical framework into action. It is a default workflow, not a method for controlling another person's reaction.

## Step 1 — Diagnose why the current arrangement persists

Possible explanations include:

- The arrangement benefits the other party.
- Changing it costs more than continuing.
- The request is unclear.
- The parties disagree about the facts or desired outcome.
- The person lacks the resources or ability to comply.
- The issue is being avoided.
- There is no meaningful consequence for inaction.
- Habit, emotional reactivity, or misunderstanding sustains the pattern.

Do not jump straight to manipulation as the explanation.

**Key structural insight:** If staying still is cheaper than moving, there may be little incentive to change. This describes a possible incentive structure, not proof of intent.

## Step 2 — Improve your own alternatives

Before increasing pressure, determine whether you can reduce the dependency that makes the situation costly.

Develop alternatives, preserve your own records, obtain independent information, establish a fallback plan, and avoid relying entirely on informal promises.

Ask whether the change improves your position even if the other party does nothing.

## Step 3 — Make the requested change concrete

Specify the desired outcome, relevant facts, terms for resolution, a reasonable timeframe if needed, how completion will be verified, and your boundary if the issue remains unresolved.

Example:

"Here is the specific issue. This is the change I am requesting. Please tell me whether you can meet these terms by the agreed date. If not, I will use the alternative available to me."

A boundary describes what you will do; it does not guarantee what the other person will do.

## Step 4 — Address inconsistencies without forcing a confession

Identify what was said, what happened, why the two appear inconsistent, and what explanations remain possible.

Invite clarification where appropriate. A person does not have to accept your characterization of their motives for a practical resolution to occur.

The objective is a clearer decision or verifiable change—not the emotional satisfaction of making someone admit defeat.

## Step 5 — State your position, then stop unnecessary repetition

Once the request, terms, and boundary are clear, repeated explanation may add little value.

Pause when further discussion produces no new information or progress. Allow the other party to respond while you decide what to do next.

Do not confuse silence with proof of discomfort, concession, guilt, or compliance.

## Step 6 — Respond proportionately if the interaction escalates

Do not match hostility automatically. Clarify the immediate issue if useful and safe, end an unproductive or unsafe interaction, preserve relevant communications accurately, and seek appropriate support when warranted.

An outburst may be evidence of a behavior. It is not proof of every allegation or of a calculated strategy.

Do not provoke an outburst to manufacture evidence.

## Step 7 — Offer a viable path to resolution

When appropriate, make a legitimate resolution practical: clear terms, a correction process, a negotiated compromise, or a reasonable way to fulfill an obligation.

A face-saving option is useful when it helps resolve the issue without unnecessary humiliation. It should not conceal misconduct or distort the record.

You can present options. You cannot reliably control how another person interprets their choice.

## Step 8 — Follow through using legitimate consequences

If the issue remains unresolved, act according to the actual terms, obligations, and options available.

This may involve withdrawing voluntary cooperation, choosing an alternative arrangement, renegotiating, using an appropriate complaint process, obtaining qualified advice, or disengaging.

Consequences should be relevant, proportionate, and within your rights and obligations.

## Integrated intervention sequence

1. Diagnose.
2. Verify.
3. Improve alternatives.
4. Specify the requested outcome.
5. Communicate and separate competing issues.
6. Pause.
7. Assess the actual response.
8. Follow through.
9. Reassess.
10. Stop when further action no longer serves the objective.

Safety, urgency, legal obligations, and new evidence can require a different sequence.

# Part IV — Integration and decision-making

## Layer 17 — Unified Incentive–Dependency–Leverage Audit

**Core question: Given the complete analysis, what do the facts justify doing?**

Consolidate the earlier layers:

- What is established, uncertain, inferred, or unsupported?
- What does each party want?
- What does each party depend on?
- What can each party realistically do?
- Why might the current arrangement persist?
- Which explanation best fits the evidence?
- What alternatives remain plausible?
- Which action advances the legitimate objective at an acceptable cost?
- What would justify stopping or changing course?

**Output:** Concise situation model, viable options, preferred action, confidence level, and stopping conditions.

## Layer 18 — The Overthinking Test

**Core question: Am I analyzing this because more analysis will improve my decision, or because I haven't accepted uncertainty?**

Ask:

1. Has new evidence appeared?
2. Am I repeating the same evidence under different labels?
3. Can the evidence distinguish my competing hypotheses?
4. What information would actually change my decision?
5. Have I established a stopping condition?

**Override:** If further analysis has no meaningful expected value, stop and execute the best proportionate decision available.

## Layer 19 — The Competence Audit

**Core question: What independent evidence demonstrates that my interpretation is accurate?**

Examine whether you remember successes more readily than failures, rationalize incorrect predictions, mistake influence for understanding, explain behavior only after it occurs, or attribute strategy where ordinary explanations suffice.

### Calibration protocol

1. Record predictions before outcomes occur.
2. State the reasoning and confidence level.
3. Compare predictions with actual outcomes.
4. Track confident failures.
5. Separate correct reasoning from lucky outcomes.
6. Identify situations where judgment performs poorly.
7. Revise confidence based on demonstrated accuracy.

**Override:** Confidence is not a substitute for demonstrated accuracy.

## Layer 20 — Identity Audit

**Core question: Based on verified evidence and repeated behavior, what can I reasonably conclude about who this person is?**

Distinguish claimed identity, demonstrated values, repeated behavioral patterns, behavior under pressure, accountability, and capacity for change.

A single incident supports a narrow conclusion about that incident. A repeated, corroborated pattern across relevant contexts can support a stronger behavioral characterization.

For example, "repeatedly redirects accountability discussions toward the complainant's conduct" is more defensible than an unsupported claim about the person's entire character.

**Output:** Defensible behavioral characterization, confidence level, counterevidence, and unresolved questions.

## Layer 21 — Relational Drivers and Behavioral Elicitation

**Core question: Which emotional or relational drivers may influence behavior, and what does an observable response actually establish?**

Assess seven possible drivers:

1. **Affection:** Warmth, care, and protectiveness.
2. **Attachment:** Connection, security, proximity, and reassurance.
3. **Identity:** Self-concept, values, status, and belonging.
4. **Loyalty:** Commitment to a person, group, principle, or shared history.
5. **Habit:** Learned behavior reinforced through repetition and cues.
6. **Grief:** Responses to loss, absence, or changed relationships.
7. **Love:** A complex bond that may combine care, attachment, commitment, desire, shared identity, and responsibility.

These drivers overlap and are hypotheses, not reliable mechanisms for producing a predetermined response.

### Behavioral elicitation sequence

1. Form a driver hypothesis.
2. Identify a relevant natural context or respectful question.
3. Observe the response.
4. Assess competing explanations.
5. Check whether your intervention influenced the response.

Eliciting information is different from engineering distress. A reaction you provoked is not independent confirmation of your theory.

**Output:** Driver hypothesis, observed response, competing explanations, confidence, and verification plan.

## Layer 22 — Timing and Escalation Audit

**Core question: When does the behavior occur, what precedes it, and what does the timing actually establish?**

Map the circumstances in which the disputed behavior appears.

Examine:

- What happened immediately before the interaction?
- Was there a request for accountability, a disputed fact, a perceived rejection, a change in status, or a request for information?
- Does the behavior recur under similar circumstances?
- Does it occur with other people or only in this relationship?
- What changes when the discussion becomes specific or verifiable?
- What de-escalates the situation?
- What role do your own questions, tone, persistence, or withdrawal play?

For DARVO-like behavior, note whether the original allegation repeatedly disappears when accountability is requested.

For elicitation concerns, note whether questioning repeatedly focuses on your knowledge, chronology, sources, or private feelings without a clear purpose.

These are observations to test, not universal diagnostic rules.

### Escalation analysis

Distinguish:

- **Trigger:** What preceded the behavior?
- **Behavior:** What actually occurred?
- **Consequence:** What happened afterward?
- **Reinforcement:** Did the behavior obtain attention, end the discussion, delay a decision, or change the subject?
- **Alternative explanation:** What else could account for the sequence?

A pattern in which a topic is repeatedly abandoned after an outburst may help explain why the behavior persists. It does not by itself prove that the outburst was consciously planned.

### Response principle

Use calm, specific, procedural communication where appropriate. If direct questioning predictably escalates an unsafe situation, prioritize safety and outside support over continuing the analysis.

**Output:** Trigger map, chronology, alternative explanations, and a response plan.

## Layer 23 — Stopping Conditions and Exit

**Core question: What would make continued engagement no longer worth the cost?**

Define success, failure, and exit conditions before a high-stakes interaction where possible.

### Success criteria

Use observable outcomes rather than relying solely on an admission of fault.

- **Minimum:** The original question receives a clear, relevant response.
- **Acceptable:** The disputed issue is addressed through an agreed process, verifiable terms, or a justified decision to stop.
- **Ideal:** The underlying problem is resolved and the relevant behavior changes sustainably.

Acknowledgment may matter, particularly where accountability or relationship repair is the objective. It should not be treated as the only possible evidence of resolution.

### Failure criteria

Examples include:

- The original issue repeatedly remains unaddressed.
- Relevant terms are repeatedly changed without justification.
- No new information emerges despite repeated discussion.
- Costs rise without meaningful progress.
- The interaction becomes unsafe.
- You begin using the same distortions or pressure tactics you object to.
- The situation increasingly depends on obtaining a confession rather than resolving the actual problem.

### Exit criteria

Specify what would justify pausing, reducing contact, changing the process, seeking independent help, or leaving the arrangement.

Assess the actual costs and risks of each option. Exit can be valuable, but it is not automatically easy or safe.

### Exit is not omnipotence

The ability to disengage can reduce dependence on another person's cooperation. It does not guarantee control over the outcome, remove all obligations, or eliminate consequences.

The relevant question is not "Who can leave most cheaply?" in isolation. It is "Which available option best protects legitimate interests at an acceptable cost?"

**Output:** Written success criteria, failure criteria, exit options, and a stopping rule.

# Part V — Three global trump cards

These overrides can interrupt any layer.

## Trump Card 1 — Overthinking

Am I analyzing this because more analysis will improve my decision, or because I haven't accepted uncertainty?

- Has new evidence appeared?
- Has my confidence or available action changed?
- Is the analysis producing testable predictions or merely more interpretations?
- What condition tells me to stop?

**Override:** If further analysis has no meaningful expected value, stop.

## Trump Card 2 — Competence

What independent evidence demonstrates that my interpretation is accurate?

- Have I made predictions before seeing outcomes?
- Do I track confident failures?
- Could I be rationalizing an incorrect prediction?
- Would the same reasoning convince me if someone else presented it?
- What safeguards protect me if my judgment is wrong?

**Override:** Downgrade conclusions that exceed the evidence.

## Trump Card 3 — Identity

Based on verified evidence and repeated behavior, what can I reasonably conclude about who this person is?

- What conduct is established?
- What patterns repeat across contexts?
- What counterevidence exists?
- Am I describing demonstrated behavior or claiming to know someone's inner essence?

**Override:** Make the strongest characterization the evidence supports—but no stronger.

# Part VI — Final decision gate

## The six-question checklist

1. **What do I actually know?** Separate verified facts from inferences, hypotheses, assumptions, and unknowns.
2. **What does everyone want?** Map objectives, incentives, competing priorities, and plausible motives.
3. **What does everyone depend on?** Identify resources, relationships, obligations, alternatives, and exit costs.
4. **What can everyone realistically do?** Assess capabilities, leverage, constraints, likely costs, and alternatives.
5. **What action best serves my objective at acceptable cost?** Compare effectiveness, risk, reversibility, proportionality, and ethical constraints.
6. **Is this genuinely in my self-interest, or will it bite me in the ass later?** Consider delayed consequences, retaliation, legal and reputational exposure, damaged relationships, new dependencies, opportunity costs, and whether short-term victory undermines the larger objective.

### Final check

**Would this still be a reasonable action if my interpretation of the other person were wrong?**

## The three tests of action

- **Capability:** Can I do it?
- **Self-interest:** Is it likely to improve my position over the relevant time horizon?
- **Justification:** Is it proportionate, defensible, and consistent with my obligations and values?

Passing the first test does not guarantee passing the other two.

# Governing principles

1. Incentives explain possible motives; they do not prove intent.
2. Dependencies explain constraints; they do not prove exploitation.
3. Leverage represents potential influence; it does not guarantee control.
4. Information asymmetry creates uncertainty; missing information does not prove concealment.
5. Context includes conditions you may have helped create.
6. Elicitation produces information to evaluate, not reactions that automatically establish truth.
7. Theories must survive alternative explanations and contradictory evidence.
8. Your own incentives, dependencies, leverage, and errors receive the same scrutiny as everyone else's.
9. Human behavior can be irrational, emotional, habitual, relational, or unstrategic.
10. Identity-level conclusions require stronger evidence than descriptions of isolated behavior.
11. Analysis must have stopping conditions.
12. Confidence must be calibrated against demonstrated accuracy.
13. A reaction you helped produce is not independent confirmation of your theory.
14. Reducing dependency, improving alternatives, documenting facts, negotiating, using legitimate procedures, and disengaging can be more effective than increasing pressure.
15. The fact that someone has not changed does not establish why they have not changed.
16. A clear boundary is more useful when paired with a realistic plan to follow through.
17. Legitimate consequences are different from manufactured crises.
18. Anger, silence, defensiveness, and concession are not standalone measures of truth.
19. The other party's freedom to choose remains real; you can structure your options, not guarantee their response.
20. A strategy must be evaluated by its actual outcome and cost, not by how sophisticated it appears.
21. DARVO is a behavioral pattern to evaluate, not a label that automatically settles a dispute.
22. Elicitation is not inherently malicious; assess its purpose, context, and use.
23. Short answers and limited disclosure are tools for maintaining boundaries, not universal rules.
24. Written records improve traceability but do not automatically establish truth.
25. A pattern is stronger when repeated, independently corroborated, and assessed against alternative explanations.
26. The ability to exit is an option whose value depends on its real costs and consequences.
27. Power is neutral. Its use has consequences.
28. Just because you can doesn't mean you should.

# Master question

> If every motive, theory, narrative, and psychological explanation disappeared, what facts would still remain—and given the actual incentives, dependencies, leverage, constraints, timing, and human drivers involved, what is the lowest-risk way to achieve the legitimate objective?

# Final standard

The framework succeeds when it improves the quality of the decision—even when that means rejecting the initial theory, recognizing that analysis has gone too far, admitting that your judgment may be wrong, reducing your own dependency, choosing a procedural response over a tactical maneuver, or deciding not to exercise available power.

The objective is not maximum leverage. It is **sound judgment, effective action, defensible consequences, and a decision that remains reasonable even when your interpretation is imperfect.**

**Just because I can doesn't mean I should. Power is neutral.**
